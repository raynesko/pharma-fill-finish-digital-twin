## 60-second pitch (142 words; read it aloud with a timer, target 75 s or less)
> Sterile fill-finish lines are expensive, and improvements like a second inspection machine are often justified with averages that ignore queues. I built a simulation of a vial fill-finish line, with breakdowns, changeovers, staff and QA release, from published benchmarks, because I had no plant data. I tested it against queueing theory, froze a baseline at 57% OEE, and wrote my hypotheses down before running any scenario. The constraint was the filler, and changeovers ate 27% of its time, so cutting changeovers in half gave about 25% more good vials, while a second inspection machine did nothing, because inspection was not the constraint. Three of my own hypotheses were wrong, and those were the most useful findings: for example, an extra operator mattered because the waits were at the packaging end. With real data I would fit downtime from the historian and recalibrate.

## Interview Q&A (operations hiring manager)
| Question | Talking points |
|---|---|
| What was the bottleneck, and was it stable? | The filler, by utilization, active-period, blocking/starvation and queue methods (all 4 agree, every scenario). It is the sole bottleneck only 62% of the time: failures and changeovers make it shift, and the cartoner is a secondary bottleneck. No scenario moved it. |
| The filler runs only 57% of scheduled time. How is it the constraint? | Its lost time is its own changeovers, not waiting: it is almost never starved or blocked. Utilization alone undersells it, which is why I used several methods. |
| Why did a second AVI machine not help? | Theory of Constraints: capacity at a non-constraint adds WIP, not output. Result +0.2% (CI -0.1 to +0.6%). It did shorten the queue in front of AVI by 2.2 min. |
| How would SMED apply? | Separate internal from external setup. The -25%/-50% levels are assumptions from a published case study, not measured; they gave 11.1% and 24.8%. Shorten changeovers at every station: downstream changeovers also block the filler. |
| Why not make batches smaller for flexibility? | In the model halving batches cost 16.9% output and added 27 h of lead time, because QA release queueing grew 34 h. Smaller batches move the lead-time constraint to QA. |
| How do you know the simulation is right? | Verification, not validation: 222 tests including vial conservation, state-time accounting and analytic checks within 1.2% of theory. Validation is face validity only; no plant data. |
| Why paired comparisons? | Common random numbers give each scenario the same breakdowns as the baseline, so the comparison isolates the change. CIs were 1.3x to 7.1x narrower than unpaired ones. |
| Which of your hypotheses were wrong? | H2b, H3a, H4a (refuted in whole or in part). For example, I expected staffing to matter only where the filler waited for staff; the waits were downstream. Pre-registering made that visible instead of letting me rationalize it. |
| Did you find bugs? | Two, both from checks: parallel AVI units let a batch's last sub-lot overtake (spurious changeovers), and station reject counts were logged after the quality step. Both were fixed, documented as errata, and every run was repeated. |
| Where did the numbers come from? | A benchmark source table with a confidence grade per value; judgment values are tagged. With real data I would fit time-to-failure and repair from the historian or MES and calibrate to measured OEE. |
| What GMP issues touch the model? | Line clearance at changeovers (21 CFR 211.130(e) for packaging and labeling; fill-area citation is VERIFY), QA batch release, Grade A interventions (EU GMP Annex 1, clause VERIFY). A model like this is decision support, not a validated GxP system. |
| What are the limits? | One line, liquid vials, one week and product mix, no lyophilization, sterility-test holds or deviations, no costs. Ranking is by output, not ROI. |
