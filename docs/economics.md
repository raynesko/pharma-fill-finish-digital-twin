Extra vials per year = (paired gain in good vials per scheduled hour) x 70 h x 48 weeks, assuming the baseline weekly campaign (6 batches, same mix) repeats every week. Payback and net value use the **lower end of the 95% CI** of the gain (a cautious reading) and the mid cost. The worst case combines the high cost, the $0.50 margin, the CI low gain and 44 weeks.

## What this shows (and what it does not)
- **Changeover reduction pays back fast at any of the placeholder prices**, if the reduction is achieved (the -25% and -50% levels are ASSUMPTIONS, not measured SMED results). At the mid cost, a -50% SMED program pays back in 1.4 months even at $0.50 per vial; it needs only $0.06 per vial to pay back within a year.
- **Why paybacks look so short:** the line makes millions of vials a year, so a double-digit percentage gain is worth a lot even at cents per vial. The conclusion therefore barely depends on the placeholder prices, which is the useful finding; the months themselves are not.
- **A second inspection machine never pays back in this model**, at any price: the filler is the constraint, so extra inspection capacity adds no detectable output (95% CI -0.1% to +0.6%). This is the Theory of Constraints point in money terms: capacity at a non-constraint buys nothing.
- **One more line operator per shift** adds output (+3.5%) but carries a recurring cost; it covers that cost only above about $0.21 per vial.
- **Extra output is only worth money if it can be sold.** If demand is fixed, the same gain shows up instead as fewer scheduled hours (less overtime, or room for another product), which this note does not value.
- **Not included:** validation and qualification of a changed process (a real cost for any GMP change), downtime to implement, material and QA release costs per vial, taxes, discounting, and risk. The placeholder costs are not vendor quotes.
- **Use with real numbers:** replace the PLACEHOLDER rows with site margin and quoted costs; the RESULT rows stay as they are, still labeled simulated.
